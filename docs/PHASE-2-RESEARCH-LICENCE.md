# Phase 2 research, R2 — the licence

**This is not legal advice.** It reports what publishers and comparable
projects say in their own words, and where their words leave a question open.
Nothing here settles what licence the dataset carries; the owner does.

Written 16 September 2026 by a session that wrote neither `PHASE-2.md` nor the
research commission. Every page cited was read on 16 September 2026 unless it
says otherwise. Quotations are exact.

---

## 1. What was found, in a screen

- **The Parliament lets anyone reuse what it publishes**, including in a new
  product, commercially or not, under its own licence. It asks for one sentence
  of credit and, where possible, a link. The licence covers both copyright and
  the separate right that protects a database. That covers the fact sheets, the
  Official Report and the bill pages. The fact sheets carry no licence wording of
  their own.
- **legislation.gov.uk publishes under the Open Government Licence**, which
  grants the same freedoms on the same condition: say where it came from.
- **So the terms permit what this project does**: take facts from those sources,
  combine them, and hand them on. Both licences also cover anyone who gets the
  material second-hand, so a researcher who downloads the dataset is covered
  too, and has to credit the sources as well.
- **Three things are not clear from the terms.**
  1. The Parliament's page puts a ban on party-political and advertising use
     in two places, with a different scope in each. It does not say whether the
     ban travels with material handed on under another licence.
  2. The UK government says its own licence fits with the Creative Commons
     licences. The Parliament says only that its licence is "aligned with" the
     government's.
  3. legislation.gov.uk does not give one clear credit line for UK legislation
     that did not come from the EU.
- **Neither licence covers personal data.** The published bill records hold no
  names, but the staging sheet keeps the introducer's name as each fact sheet
  printed it.
- **Comparable datasets use four different licences**: CC BY-SA (V-Dem),
  CC BY-NC-SA (Comparative Agendas), CC BY (ParlaMint) and CC0 (ParlGov's
  archived releases). Two separate their own coding from the source material and
  license only their own.
- **The Comparative Agendas Project already publishes a "Scottish Bills,
  1999–2008" dataset.**

## 2. The findings in full

### 2.1 The Scottish Parliament: fact sheets, Official Report, bill pages

**Source**: "Copyright", `https://www.parliament.scot/about/copyright`.
It has three parts: a policy, the licence itself, and guidance on reuse.

**What it permits.** The licence grants "a worldwide, royalty-free, perpetual,
non-exclusive licence to use the information". Under it, you are free to:

> copy, publish, distribute and transmit the information
> adapt the information
> exploit the Information commercially and non-commercially, for example, by
> combining it with other Information, or by including it in your own product
> or application.

**What it covers.** "Use of copyright and database right material expressly made
available under this licence". 'Information' is defined as "information
protected by copyright or by database right (for example, literary and artistic
works, content, data and source code)". The policy section says: "Published
material may be reproduced without formal permission subject to the conditions
of the Scottish Parliament Copyright Licence."

**Who it covers.** 'You' means the person "acquiring rights in the Information
(whether the Information is obtained directly from the Licensor or otherwise)
under this licence".

**What it requires.**

> acknowledge the source of the Information by including the following
> attribution statement and, where possible, providing a link to this licence:
> (Contains information licensed under the Scottish Parliament Copyright Licence)

The policy section adds: "Where any material is republished or copied to others
or included in any application, the source of the material must be acknowledged".
Breaking this condition ends the licence "automatically".

**What it forbids.**

- Non-endorsement: "This licence does not grant you any right to use the
  information in a way that suggests any official status or that the SPCB
  endorses you or your use of the information."
- Party politics and advertising. This appears twice, with different scope:
  - policy section: "This licence does not allow you to use published material
    **provided in any format** in connection with party political purposes or in
    connection with advertising endorsement."
  - licence section, under Non-endorsement: "In particular, specific terms and
    conditions relate to the reuse of **downloadable files such as images and
    video footage** which states that they are: not to be used in connection with
    party political purposes; not to be used in connection with advertising
    endorsement."

  (Bold added, to show the difference.)

**What it does not cover.** "personal data in the Information"; information not
published or disclosed with the SPCB's consent; the SPCB's Corporate Identity (its
badge and logo); "third-party rights the SPCB is not authorised to license"; other
intellectual property; identity documents.

**No warranty.** The information is licensed "'as is'"; the SPCB "does not
guarantee the continued supply of the Information".

**Relationship to the government licence.** "This Scottish Parliament Licence is
aligned with OGLv3.0".

**Who owns the material.** The law gives copyright in work made "by or under the
direction or control of" the Scottish Parliament to the Scottish Parliamentary
Corporate Body. This includes work by its staff and recordings of its
proceedings (Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988, s.165, as modified by the
Parliamentary Copyright (Scottish Parliament) Order 1999, SI 1999/676, art. 2).
Bills themselves are
the SPCB's copyright from introduction until Royal Assent or until they fall
(s.166A).

**The fact sheets themselves.** Sessions 1 and 7, as held in `sources/factsheets/`,
were read page by page. Neither carries a copyright notice or licence statement.
Session 7 says it is "Published … by the authority of the Scottish Parliament".
Sessions 2–6 were not read page by page.

**The Official Report pages.** The meeting page read carries "© Scottish
Parliament 2026" and a link to the Copyright page, and no terms of its own.

**Open data.** The Parliament's publication scheme says its open data is
"available under an open government license" (`https://www.parliament.scot/about/information-rights/guide-to-information/class-9-open-data`).
The portal itself, `data.parliament.scot`, is built so that its notes page
cannot be read without running the page in a browser. It was not read.

### 2.2 legislation.gov.uk

**Source**: the footer of every page (read on `/help`, `/contributors`,
`/fair-use-policy`):

> All content is available under the Open Government Licence v3.0 except where
> otherwise stated. … © Crown and database right

**Who owns the material.** "Her Majesty is entitled to copyright in every Act of
Parliament, Act of the Scottish Parliament, …" (CDPA 1988 s.164(1)). The
copyright lasts 50 years from the end of the year of Royal Assent (s.164(2)(a)).
"No other copyright, or right in the nature of copyright, subsists in an Act"
(s.164(4)).

**The licence**: Open Government Licence v3.0,
`https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/doc/open-government-licence/version/3/`.
It grants the same freedoms, in the same words, as the Scottish Parliament's
licence: copy, publish, distribute, adapt, and "exploit the Information
commercially and non-commercially".

**What it requires.**

> acknowledge the source of the Information in your product or application by
> including or linking to any attribution statement specified by the Information
> Provider(s) and, where possible, provide a link to this licence;
> If the Information Provider does not provide a specific attribution statement,
> you must use the following:
> Contains public sector information licensed under the Open Government Licence v3.0.
> If you are using Information from several Information Providers and listing
> multiple attributions is not practical in your product or application, you may
> include a URI or hyperlink to a resource that contains the required attribution
> statements.

**What it does not cover**: personal data; unpublished information; logos,
crests and the Royal Arms except where they form part of a document or dataset;
third-party rights; other intellectual property; identity documents.

**Non-endorsement**: no use "that suggests any official status or that the
Information Provider and/or Licensor endorse you".

**Attribution wording.** The contributors page
(`https://www.legislation.gov.uk/contributors`) suggests one statement:
"Crown © and database right material re-used under the Open Government
Licence". It gives this for UK law derived from the EU, alongside the EU's own
wording. No page read gives a statement for UK legislation that did not
originate in the EU.

**Fair use policy** (`https://www.legislation.gov.uk/fair-use-policy`). This is
not a licence term, but it would bind any automated reading later:

- a program "must identify yourself … with a legitimate user agent string";
- it must follow `robots.txt`;
- it must stay under "1,500 requests in any 5-minute period".

### 2.3 Compatibility with other licences

**Source**: The National Archives, "Open Government Licence",
`https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/information-management/re-using-public-sector-information/uk-government-licensing-framework/open-government-licence/`:

> The OGL terms are compatible with the latest versions of the Creative Commons
> Attribution License and the Open Data Commons Attribution License. This means
> that when the information is adapted and licensed under either of those
> licences, you automatically meet the conditions of the OGL as long as you
> comply with the terms of the other licence.

This statement is about the OGL. No page read makes the same statement about the
Scottish Parliament Copyright Licence.

### 2.4 Whether dates and outcomes are protected at all

Only the statute was read here; nothing in this section applies it to this
dataset.

- **Database right** exists "in a database if there has been a substantial
  investment in obtaining, verifying or presenting the contents of the
  database", whether or not the contents are themselves copyright (Copyright and
  Rights in Databases Regulations 1997, SI 1997/3032, reg. 13).
- It is infringed by extracting or re-using "all or a substantial part of the
  contents" without consent. "Repeated and systematic extraction" of small parts
  can count as a substantial part (reg. 16).
- The research exception covers fair dealing where a part "is extracted … for
  the purpose of illustration for teaching or research and not for any
  commercial purpose, and … the source is indicated" (reg. 20(1)). It names
  extraction. It does not name re-use, which is the making available to the
  public.
- A database is protected by copyright as a literary work "if, and only if, by
  reason of the selection or arrangement of the contents … the database
  constitutes the author's own intellectual creation" (CDPA s.3A(2)).

Both source licences expressly license database right as well as copyright
(§2.1, §2.2). Whether a right exists in the sources therefore decides whether
the licence is needed, not whether it is given.

### 2.6 What Creative Commons advises for data

**Source**: Creative Commons FAQ, `https://creativecommons.org/faq/`.

- **Version 4.0 covers database right**: "The most recent version (4.0) may be
  used to license databases subject to copyright and, where applicable, sui
  generis database rights." Under version 3.0, the licence conditions do not
  apply "where sui generis database rights–but not copyright–are implicated".
- **On non-commercial and no-derivatives licences**: "CC does not recommend use
  of its NonCommercial (NC) or NoDerivatives (ND) licenses on databases intended
  for scholarly or scientific use."
- **On CC0**: "In certain domains, such as science and government, there are
  important reasons to consider using CC0. Waiving copyright and related rights
  eliminates all uncertainty for potential users, encouraging maximal reuse and
  sharing of information."
- **On material from others**:

  > Before making a database available under a CC license, a database provider
  > should first make sure she has all rights necessary to do so. Often, the
  > database provider is not the original author of the database contents. If
  > that is the case, the database provider should secure separate permission
  > from the other author(s) before publishing the database under a CC legal
  > tool. If a database maker decides to license the database without securing
  > permission from the author(s) of the database contents, it should clearly
  > indicate the material for which permission has not been secured and clearly
  > mark the material as not being offered under the terms of the license.

- **On licensing only part**: licensors "can choose to license some rather than
  all of the rights they have in a database. Creative Commons advises against
  this practice. However, if a licensor chooses to do so anyway, we strongly
  encourage licensors to clearly demarcate what is and is not licensed."
- **Open Data Commons licences** "apply only to sui generis database rights and
  any copyright in the database structure, they do not apply to the individual
  contents of the database."

### 2.7 What attaching a licence involves in practice

**Stating it.** Creative Commons, "Marking your work with a CC license"
(`https://wiki.creativecommons.org/wiki/Marking_your_work_with_a_CC_license`):

- "Make sure to name the specific CC license the material is under and link to
  it … Don't just say the material is Creative Commons".
- Use the licence chooser's machine-readable code on web pages.
- For a document shared offline, "use a title and/or copyright page to include
  the copyright notice and CC license information". "If you link to the document
  on a web page, make sure that the license information is clearly displayed next
  to the file".
- "if your work incorporates third party materials, you would note those
  materials and make sure to attribute each of them correctly."

**Crediting several sources.** Creative Commons, "Recommended practices for
attribution" (`https://wiki.creativecommons.org/wiki/Recommended_practices_for_attribution`):

- credit Title, Author, Source and Licence ("TASL") for each work;
- "it is clear which attribution belongs to which work";
- for some media, "consider publishing a web page with attribution information".

The OGL allows the same: a link "to a resource that contains the required
attribution statements" where listing them all is not practical (§2.2).

**What a download carries in practice.** V-Dem's ZIP carries the codebook and
suggested citations, and states the licence on its web pages. The Comparative
Agendas Project states the licence on one site-wide page and sends reuse terms
for source material to the codebooks. R1 covers what travels with a download in
full.

## 3. Where practice disagrees

- **Share-alike.** V-Dem, the Comparative Agendas Project and TheyWorkForYou's
  own lists require anything built from the data to carry the same licence.
  ParlaMint and ParlGov's archived release do not. None gives a reason.
- **Commercial use.** The Comparative Agendas Project forbids it. The Scottish
  Parliament, the OGL, V-Dem, ParlaMint and ParlGov's archived release allow it.
  Creative Commons advises against non-commercial licences on scholarly
  databases; the UK Data Service accepts them for open data.
- **Attribution or none.** ParlGov's archived release waives every right (CC0).
  Everyone else requires credit. Creative Commons gives reasons for both.
- **Licensing the whole or only your own part.** The Comparative Agendas Project
  and TheyWorkForYou license only what they made, and leave source material under
  its original terms. V-Dem and ParlaMint license the whole dataset. Creative
  Commons advises against licensing only part, but says that if it is done, the
  line must be clearly drawn.
- **Stated, or not stated.** ParlGov's own site and the Congressional Bills
  Project's listing ask for a citation and state no licence.

## 5. Questions only the owner can answer

1. **Whether to put the open questions to the publishers**, below. Writing to
   either is contacting an outside body, which is yours to decide.
2. **Commercial use.** Whether someone may use the dataset commercially. The
   sources allow it; one comparable dataset forbids it.
3. **Share-alike.** Whether anything built from the dataset must carry the same
   licence.
4. **The whole or only our part.** Whether the licence covers the whole dataset,
   or only what the project made (its coding, the derived columns, the
   methodology notes), with the source facts left under their own licences.
5. **The PhD dataset's terms**, as far as any of its values reach a download.
6. **The staging sheet.** Whether it is ever published. It holds the
   introducer's name as each fact sheet printed it, and both source licences
   exclude personal data.
7. **The 2017 licence.** Whether to read the archived Scottish Parliament Licence
   2017 through your browser.

## 6. Recommendation

*A recommendation, kept apart from the findings. It settles nothing.*

- **License what the project made under CC BY 4.0.** It is the licence The
  National Archives names as compatible with the OGL. It covers database right,
  where version 3.0 did not. It is what the UK Data Service prefers for data that
  is not Crown copyright. And it keeps the credit chain that "provenance reaches
  the reader" depends on, which CC0 would give up. Avoid non-commercial, for the
  reason Creative Commons gives. Leave share-alike unless you want it; nothing
  read gives a reason for it.
- **Carry both source statements with every download and on one page of the
  site**, each saying which columns it covers: the Scottish Parliament's
  sentence and link, and the OGL's. That is what both licences and Creative
  Commons' guidance ask, and what the OGL's "link to a resource" provision
  allows.
- **Say plainly on the site that the resource is not the Parliament's**, since
  both licences forbid suggesting official status.
- **Send the SPCB question before the first public download**, rather than
  settling its two readings ourselves. The legislation.gov.uk question is
  smaller: using the longer "Crown © and database right" wording would likely
  satisfy either reading, but asking costs little.
- **Keep the introducers' names out of any download** unless that is decided
  separately. They fall outside both licences.
